# AI Lawyer Test Cases: 20 Scenarios

Case descriptions and evidence descriptions for testing an AI legal assistant. All cases are jurisdiction-neutral, and all names and facts are fictional.

## Case 1: Sexual assault (criminal)

### Case description

Meera Nair, 24, a junior analyst, attended a colleague's farewell party at a rented venue on a Saturday night. She says she drank two glasses of wine and, around 11:30 pm, began feeling unusually dizzy and disoriented. A senior colleague, Rohan Malhotra, 38, offered to put her in a cab. According to Meera, she has only fragmented memories after that. She recalls the car stopping at an apartment building, not her own, and waking the next morning in an unfamiliar bedroom with Rohan beside her, her clothing disarranged, and no memory of consenting to anything. She left immediately and went home.

Meera did not report the incident that day. She spoke to a friend on Sunday evening and went to a hospital on Monday afternoon, about 40 hours after the event, after the friend urged her to. She filed a police complaint on Wednesday, five days after the incident, and says the delay was due to shock, fear of losing her job, and uncertainty about whether what she remembered "counted." Rohan admits she came to his apartment and that they had sexual contact, but claims it was fully consensual. He says Meera was "tipsy but talkative," initiated physical contact herself, and sent him a friendly message the next morning. Both work in the same company, and HR has been informed informally.

The legal questions are whether consent can exist when a person is intoxicated or possibly drugged, how the delayed reporting affects credibility and evidence, and whether the friendly message weakens her claim. There is also a parallel workplace-conduct angle, with possible internal disciplinary proceedings and a duty on the employer to protect the complainant. Meera wants to know what her options are, what evidence still matters, how strong the case is, and what protections she can ask for during the process. The AI must handle this with sensitivity and without victim-blaming or prejudging the accused.

### Evidence description

The medical report from Monday's hospital visit notes mild bruising on the upper arm and records Meera's account, but no toxicology sample was collected, so any drug presence is unverifiable now. A forensic examination was done at 40 hours, which may have limited recovery of biological evidence. The police complaint is dated five days after the incident and contains a detailed but partly fragmented narrative, with gaps in her memory that she openly acknowledges. The venue's CCTV shows Meera leaving with Rohan at 11:42 pm. She walks unsteadily but without being carried, and the footage ends at the venue door.

A cab-app record shows a ride booked from Rohan's phone to his address, with Meera's home address never entered. The WhatsApp thread includes a message from Rohan at 1:10 am ("You okay? Home safe?") and a message from Meera the next morning at 9:05 ("Left early, thanks for the ride") that Rohan's lawyer says shows consent. Meera says she sent it out of panic and a wish to avoid conflict. Two witnesses saw her drinking, with differing estimates of her intake, and one says she looked "very drowsy" after her second glass. The friend she confided in is a hearsay witness to her distress. No messages exist from the night itself, and Rohan's apartment building has no working camera.

## Case 2: Food safety and poisoning (civil and regulatory)

### Case description

On a Friday evening, the Kapoor family of four ordered a family platter from a mid-sized restaurant, "Spice Terrace," for a birthday dinner. Within 6 to 10 hours, all four developed severe vomiting, diarrhea, and fever. The youngest, 7-year-old Aarav, was hospitalized for two days with dehydration, and his father, Sanjay, a daily-wage contractor, lost a week of earnings. The family believes the cause was the chicken biryani and the cold dessert, since they ate nothing else common to all four that day. They have not eaten out elsewhere in the previous 48 hours.

When the family called the restaurant on Saturday morning, the manager first apologized, then denied responsibility, saying "no other customers complained." The family posted a review online, and the restaurant responded publicly that the complaint was "fabricated for compensation." Sanjay is now seeking reimbursement for medical bills, lost wages, and compensation for suffering, and wants the restaurant inspected. The restaurant's owner has threatened a defamation claim over the review. The family also wants to know whether they should complain to the food-safety authority, and what to do first.

The legal issues include proving causation between the meal and the illness, since the family kept no leftovers. They also include the restaurant's duty of care and liability for negligent food handling, quantifying the damages, and the interplay between a civil claim, a regulatory complaint, and the defamation threat. The AI should explain what evidence is strong and weak, how to preserve what remains, whether a collective claim or a consumer forum is the right route, and how to answer the defamation threat without escalating.

### Evidence description

The family has the online order confirmation and payment receipt showing the exact dishes, timestamp, and amount, along with the delivery photo. Hospital discharge summaries for Aarav and outpatient prescriptions for the other three note "acute gastroenteritis, probable food-borne origin," with stool culture ordered for Aarav only, results pending. Sanjay's employer has issued a letter confirming 6 days of lost work. A neighbor says Sanjay's wife mentioned the biryani "smelled slightly off" that night, but this is secondhand and she is not sure.

No food samples exist because the family threw away the leftovers. Two other customers have since commented on the review thread saying they were sick after eating there the same week, but they are anonymous and unverified. A screenshot of the manager's apology call, saved as a call log with a 2-minute duration, shows contact but no content, and no recording exists. The restaurant's most recent food-safety inspection report is public and shows a "minor violations" rating from 8 months ago, including temperature-control issues in cold storage. The restaurant's legal notice threatening defamation is dated Monday.

## Case 3: Consumer protection, e-commerce (consumer)

### Case description

Priya Menon bought a 55-inch smart TV for 62,000 from an online marketplace during a "festival sale," selling through a third-party seller. The listing stated "1-year brand warranty + 7-day replacement." The TV arrived with a minor box dent but worked for 19 days. On day 20, a vertical line appeared on the screen, followed by complete panel failure a week later. She raised a complaint on the platform, which routed her to the seller, who said the panel damage was "physical" and not covered. The brand's service center sent a technician who, after a brief visit, marked the issue as "customer damage, warranty void" without opening the device.

Priya argues the damage is a manufacturing defect that appeared within the warranty, and that the seller and the platform both owe her a remedy. The seller says the 7-day replacement window has lapsed, the brand says warranty is void, and the platform says it is only an intermediary. Priya has spent six weeks on calls and chats, receiving contradictory responses from each party. She now wants a refund or replacement, plus compensation for the harassment, and asks who is actually legally responsible and which forum she should go to.

The key issues are the liability of each of the three parties, whether the platform can really disclaim responsibility, how an unsupported "customer damage" finding should be treated, and whether the dented box matters. The AI should also assess whether a legal notice is worth sending first, whether the claim value justifies a formal consumer complaint, and what remedies are realistically available. It should also explain how limitation and warranty period work here.

### Evidence description

Priya has the order invoice, the seller's tax invoice, the product listing screenshot showing the warranty and replacement terms, and the delivery confirmation with a timestamp. She took an unboxing video on delivery day that shows the box dent but also a working screen on first power-on, which is helpful for her. She has photos of the screen showing the vertical line and the later complete failure, with file timestamps that match her account. The platform's chat transcripts and her email threads show a pattern of being passed between parties, with the platform's agent at one point writing that "the seller will resolve this."

The technician's service report states "physical damage suspected, warranty void" but includes no photographs, no inspection checklist, and no description of visible impact marks. Priya has no independent expert opinion yet. The warranty card was not in the box, and the brand's website says the warranty is registered automatically on purchase, a claim she has not been able to confirm. There is no evidence of any drop or mishandling, although the delivery courier's handover photo shows the box being carried loosely.

## Case 4: Wrongful termination and workplace harassment (employment)

### Case description

Arjun Das, 31, worked for four years as a senior sales manager at a logistics software company, with consistently positive reviews and a "top performer" award two years ago. Eight months ago, a new regional director, Vikram Sethi, took over and began making comments about Arjun's "age and slow adaptation," excluding him from meetings, and reassigning his key accounts to a junior colleague. Arjun raised the issue verbally with HR twice and then sent a formal written complaint on 3 March, alleging discriminatory treatment and a hostile environment.

Eleven days later, he was placed on a "performance improvement plan" with targets that Arjun says were unachievable after losing his accounts. On 2 May, he was terminated by email for "poor performance and failure to meet PIP objectives," with one month's salary in lieu of notice and a request to sign a settlement agreement waiving all claims within seven days. Arjun believes the termination was retaliation for his complaint, and he is also unsure whether to sign the settlement, whether his unvested stock options are lost, and whether the "age" comments qualify as discrimination.

The central issues are retaliation (the timing between complaint and PIP), pretext in the performance case, the strength of evidence for discrimination, and the contractual terms on notice, stock options, and the non-disparagement clause in the settlement. The AI should evaluate the claim's strengths and weaknesses honestly, advise on the settlement deadline and negotiation leverage, identify which forum and limitation periods apply, and explain what Arjun should and should not do now, such as not deleting emails or sharing company documents improperly.

### Evidence description

Arjun has his employment contract, which includes a 30-day notice clause, a stock vesting schedule, and a confidentiality clause. He has four years of performance reviews, the award certificate, and the written complaint to HR with a delivery receipt dated 3 March. The PIP document dated 14 March lists three targets, two of which depend on accounts that had already been reassigned. An email from Vikram to a junior team member says "move his big accounts to you, he's not keeping up with the new tools," sent before the PIP, which Arjun was accidentally copied on.

The termination email gives no specifics on how the targets were missed. HR has not replied in writing to the complaint beyond an acknowledgment. Arjun has WhatsApp messages from two colleagues saying they heard Vikram describe him as "old school," but both say they don't want to be named. He has no recordings and no written record of the verbal comments, only his own diary notes made a few weeks later. He has not yet forwarded any company files to his personal email, which is a point in his favor, and the settlement draft gives him seven days to sign.

## Case 5: Inheritance and property dispute (civil, family)

### Case description

Lakshmi Reddy, 68, owned a house and a small plot of agricultural land. She died 14 months ago, leaving three adult children: Suresh, who lived with her and cared for her in her final years, Anita, who lives abroad, and Kiran, who lives in another city. Two weeks before her death, while she was in hospital, a will was reportedly signed in Suresh's favor, leaving him the house and the land, with a small cash sum for each sibling. Anita and Kiran learned about the will only after the funeral and are shocked because Lakshmi had told both of them for years that the property would be divided equally.

Anita and Kiran suspect the will was signed when Lakshmi was too ill and medicated to understand it, and that Suresh influenced her. Suresh responds that he did everything for his mother while the others "visited twice a year," that she freely chose to reward him, and that the will was properly witnessed. He has already begun the process of mutating the property in his name and has put the land on the market. Anita and Kiran want to know whether they can contest the will, whether they can stop the sale immediately, and what their realistic chances are.

The legal questions are testamentary capacity, undue influence, due execution and witnessing, and whether there is any earlier will or intestacy rule that would give each child an equal share. There is also an urgency issue, since the land sale could complete before any court acts. The AI should explain the burden of proof, the value of interim relief such as an injunction, the limitation period for challenging a will, and what evidence Anita and Kiran should gather quickly, while being fair to Suresh's caregiving role.

### Evidence description

The will is a typed one-page document dated 9 days before Lakshmi's death, signed with a shaky signature and witnessed by two people: the hospital's administrative clerk and Suresh's friend. It was not registered. Hospital records show Lakshmi was admitted for kidney failure and was receiving sedatives and pain medication, and a nursing note from the day before the will records her as "intermittently confused, oriented to person only." There is no doctor's certificate of capacity on the day of signing.

An earlier handwritten note from 6 years ago, found by Anita in a family album, says "all three children should share equally," but it is unwitnessed and unsigned beyond initials. Suresh's bank statements show he paid most of the hospital bills, which supports his caregiving claim. A cousin states that Suresh asked Lakshmi to "sign something" in hospital and she "didn't seem to know what it was," but the cousin was not present for the signing. The property listing for the land is already live online, and the municipal record shows Suresh's mutation application filed three weeks ago, still pending.

## Case 6: Trademark conflict (intellectual property)

### Case description

Nandini Rao runs "Kaveri Kitchen," a small home-based packaged-spice business that began selling at local markets in 2019 and moved to online sales in 2021. She used a distinctive logo, a stylized copper pot with a leaf, on her packaging, social media, and market stall banners. She never registered the trademark because she believed her business was too small to need it. In March this year, she received a legal notice from "Kaveri Foods Pvt. Ltd.," a larger company, claiming that her use of the logo infringes its registered trademark, which was filed in 2024. The notice demands that she stop using the logo, destroy her packaging, and pay a penalty.

Nandini says she used the logo years before the company even applied. She also says the two logos are similar only in the use of a pot, while hers has a leaf and a different color scheme, and that her customers have never confused the two brands. The company's notice gives her 15 days to comply. She is frightened about losing her packaging stock, which she has just reordered in bulk. She wants to know whether prior use gives her any rights, whether she can challenge the company's registration, whether she should rebrand, and how to respond to the notice.

The legal issues include the rights of an unregistered prior user, the difference between registered and common-law protection, the likelihood of consumer confusion, geographic and market overlap, and possible opposition or cancellation proceedings and their deadlines. The AI should assess how strong her proof of prior use is, which defensive steps are worth taking now, whether to file her own application, and what a measured reply to the notice looks like, without admitting infringement.

### Evidence description

Nandini has dated photographs of her 2019 market stall showing the logo on the banner, and 2020 invoices from a local printer for logo-printed labels. She has Instagram posts from 2020 onward showing the packaging, and online order records from 2021 with sales figures. Her total sales are modest, mostly within one region, and some early invoices are handwritten without the business name. A customer testimonial from 2020 mentions the logo by description but not by image.

The company's registration certificate is dated 2024, covers "spices and condiments," and its mark is a stylized pot without a leaf in red and gold. Its notice includes side-by-side images but also a claim of "pan-national reputation" with no supporting documents. A search of the trademark register shows no earlier filings by Nandini and no other similar marks in the same class. There is no evidence of actual confusion, no complaints or misdirected orders, and the company's sales are mainly through large retail chains that Nandini does not use.

## Case 7: Defamation through an online review (civil/media)

### Case description

Dr. Farhan Siddiqui owns a mid-sized dental clinic. Six weeks ago, a patient identified only as "Rahul K." posted a one-star review on a maps platform, writing that Dr. Siddiqui "performed an unnecessary root canal to make money," "uses unsterilized tools," and "is a fraud who should lose his license." Within two weeks, the clinic's average rating dropped from 4.7 to 4.1, and Dr. Siddiqui says new-patient bookings fell sharply, with three patients cancelling and citing the review.

Dr. Siddiqui has no record of a patient named Rahul K. with a root canal, though he does have a patient named Rahul Khanna, who had a filling six months ago and a billing dispute over a missed appointment fee. He wants to know whether he can sue for defamation, force the platform to reveal the reviewer's identity, get the review removed, and recover lost income. His lawyer friend has told him that truth is a defense, that opinion is protected, and that suing might make the review go viral. He is torn between legal action and a calm public reply.

The key issues are separating factual allegations (unsterilized tools, unnecessary procedure) from opinion ("fraud"), whether the reviewer's identity can be established and compelled, the platform's takedown process, and how to prove actual loss. The AI should also weigh the Streisand-effect risk, recommend a safe public response, explain the evidence needed to show falsity, and note that the dentist should not retaliate against or contact the suspected patient.

### Evidence description

Dr. Siddiqui has screenshots of the review with its timestamp, plus the clinic's rating history from the platform dashboard showing the drop. He has the booking system export showing a decline in new appointments over four weeks compared with the previous four, and three cancellation messages that mention the review. His clinic's sterilization logs and an inspection certificate from three months ago show compliance, and he has patient records showing the filling procedure for Rahul Khanna and the billing dispute.

He cannot prove that Rahul Khanna wrote the review, since the platform profile has no photo and few other reviews. The review's wording about the root canal does not match any procedure on record, which could mean it is false or that it was written by someone else entirely. A staff member says Rahul Khanna "looked angry" at the billing dispute, which is only circumstantial. No platform response has been received to Dr. Siddiqui's takedown request, and the drop in bookings could partly reflect seasonal slowdown, which the AI must not ignore.

## Case 8: Property boundary dispute (land)

### Case description

Gopal Iyer inherited a 1,800 sq. m. plot from his father in 2012 and has farmed vegetables on it since. His neighbor, Mahesh Patil, bought the adjoining plot in 2017. Last month, Mahesh built a permanent brick fence that Gopal says cuts about 3 meters into his land along a 40-meter stretch, taking roughly 120 sq. m. Mahesh says the fence follows the "old boundary" that has been in place for decades, marked by a row of coconut trees and an irrigation channel, and that Gopal's father never objected.

Gopal's sale deed describes boundaries by reference to neighbors and landmarks rather than exact measurements, and a government survey map from 1996 shows the plot as a rough polygon with different dimensions from those in the deed. Mahesh's deed uses different landmarks, and one of them, a "village footpath," no longer exists. Mahesh has told Gopal that if he wants the fence moved, he must go to court, and that he will claim the land by long possession if needed. Gopal wants the fence removed, wants to know which documents will carry the most weight, and fears that years of acquiescence by his father may now count against him.

The legal issues include the hierarchy of evidence in boundary disputes (survey records versus deeds versus conduct), the need for an official fresh survey, adverse possession and its required period, and whether the fence can be challenged on an emergency basis. The AI should assess both sides fairly, recommend gathering a licensed survey as the first step, and advise on avoiding self-help, such as tearing down the fence, which could expose Gopal to liability.

### Evidence description

Gopal has his father's sale deed from 1989, the 1996 survey map, tax receipts in the family's name since 1990, and his own inheritance record from 2012. He has dated photographs of the plot from 2015 showing the coconut trees and irrigation channel, but the photos do not clearly show a boundary line. A hand-drawn sketch by a retired village officer, signed but undated, supports Gopal's approximate dimensions.

Mahesh has his deed from 2017 and a statement from an elderly farmer who says the "old boundary" ran along the channel as long as he remembers. The channel has been re-dug at least once, so its current position may not match the original. No fresh professional survey exists, and satellite images show the coconut trees in a line but cannot establish legal boundaries. Fence construction photos from last month show Gopal standing nearby without objecting at the time, which Mahesh may use as acquiescence.

## Case 9: Child custody modification (family law)

### Case description

Anjali and Ravi Menon divorced three years ago under a consent decree giving them joint legal custody of their 8-year-old son, Aarush, with primary residence with Anjali in the city of Pune and weekend and holiday visitation with Ravi. Two months ago, Anjali received a job offer in another city 900 km away, with a significantly higher salary, and notified Ravi in writing that she plans to relocate with Aarush at the end of the school term in four months. Ravi objects and has filed a petition to modify custody, asking that Aarush remain in Pune with him if Anjali moves.

Anjali argues that the move is necessary for financial stability and that she will arrange video calls, long summer visits, and travel costs for Ravi. Ravi says he has been involved in Aarush's daily life, attending school events and medical appointments, and that the move would effectively end their relationship. Anjali claims Ravi is often late and sometimes cancels visits, while Ravi says Anjali has restricted his time on several occasions. Aarush's school counselor has reported that he is anxious about the possible change but has not expressed a preference.

The core issue is the best interests of the child, weighing stability, each parent's involvement, the reason for relocation, the feasibility of maintaining the father-child relationship, and the child's own views given his age. The AI must stay strictly neutral, avoid favoring either parent by gender, explain how courts commonly approach relocation requests, identify what each side must prove, and suggest mediation and parenting-plan options rather than adversarial litigation wherever possible.

### Evidence description

The existing custody order specifies visitation as alternate weekends and half of school holidays. Anjali's job offer letter lists the salary and a start date, and she has a housing arrangement and school admission letter for Aarush in the new city. Ravi has attendance records from parent-teacher meetings showing he went to five of the last six, and pediatric records showing him at three of four recent appointments.

Messages between the parents show two cancelled visits by Ravi in the past year, both with explanations of work travel, and three occasions when Anjali declined a swap request. One message from Anjali reads "you can see him whenever you want, just not this weekend." The school counselor's note describes Aarush as "attached to both parents" and anxious about change. No evaluation by a child psychologist exists, and neither parent has made allegations of abuse or neglect.

## Case 10: Minor criminal charge with conflicting evidence (criminal defense)

### Case description

Imran Sheikh, 22, a college student, has been charged with causing hurt and minor property damage after a fight outside a café at around 10:40 pm on a Saturday. The complainant, Deepak Rawat, says Imran punched him in the face and smashed his phone on the pavement during an argument over a parking spot. Imran admits there was an argument but says Deepak shoved him first, that he only pushed back in self-defense, and that he never touched the phone. Deepak had a cut lip and a broken phone screen, and a police complaint was filed the same night.

Imran is out on bail and worried about the effect of a record on his scholarship and future job prospects. He wants to know how strong the case against him is, whether he can claim self-defense, whether he should accept a plea or settlement offer, and how to handle the police if they call him again. He says he has not given a statement beyond his first interview, which he gave without a lawyer.

The core issues are the reliability of the witness statements, the discrepancy between the CCTV timeline and the complainant's account, the proportionality required for self-defense, and the consequences of a plea. The AI should identify inconsistencies, explain Imran's rights, advise him to retain a human defense lawyer immediately, and caution against contacting the complainant or witnesses, while avoiding any suggestion to alter or hide evidence.

### Evidence description

The charge sheet includes Deepak's statement, a medical report noting a 1 cm lip laceration, and photos of the broken phone. Deepak says the incident was at 10:40 pm, but the café's CCTV shows the physical altercation beginning at 10:52 pm, and the footage shows Deepak stepping forward and pushing Imran first, followed by Imran pushing back and Deepak falling. The phone is not visible in the footage at the moment of the alleged smashing, and the camera angle is partly blocked by a parked van.

An independent witness, a café waiter, says he saw "both men shouting" but did not see who struck first. A second witness, Deepak's friend, says Imran "attacked without warning," but the CCTV contradicts this. Imran's first police interview, given without counsel, includes a statement that he "hit him back," which the prosecution may use. No forensic evidence links Imran to the phone damage, and the phone's screen had a pre-existing crack according to a photo Deepak posted online two weeks earlier.

## Case 11: Contradictory evidence in a loan dispute (civil/debt)

### Case description

Harish Verma lent 4,50,000 to his friend and business associate Sameer Joshi in January last year, to help Sameer buy equipment for his print shop. Harish says there was a verbal understanding of repayment within 12 months. When the year ended, Sameer repaid nothing, and Harish sent a written demand. Sameer now claims that he repaid the entire amount in cash in three installments during the year, and that Harish is trying to claim twice.

Harish says he received only a small partial payment by bank transfer and nothing in cash. Sameer says he did not ask for receipts because they were close friends. Harish wants to file a recovery suit and asks the AI how strong his case is. In an early conversation, Harish also said he "might have received something in cash around June, but not sure," which is inconsistent with his later statement that he got nothing.

The issues are the burden of proof for the loan and for repayment, how courts treat claims of unrecorded cash payments, the inconsistency in Harish's own account, and the documentary trail of the transfer. The AI must not simply accept either side's story, must flag the conflict, and should ask clarifying questions before rating the case, while explaining what would corroborate or undercut each version.

### Evidence description

The bank statement shows a transfer of 4,50,000 from Harish to Sameer dated 12 January last year with the narration "loan." It also shows a single incoming transfer of 40,000 from Sameer to Harish in April. There are no other credits from Sameer in the following 14 months. Sameer's own bank statement shows three cash withdrawals of 1,50,000 each in March, June, and September, which he claims funded the cash repayments, but the statement shows he also made large equipment payments in the same weeks.

A WhatsApp thread contains Sameer writing in October "will clear the balance soon, tight on cash," which is hard to reconcile with a claim that the debt was already fully paid. Harish's own message in June reads "got the amount, thanks," but it is unclear what amount he meant. No written loan agreement or promissory note exists. A mutual friend says he heard Harish say "Sameer is paying me back slowly," which fits either version.

## Case 12: Verbal agreement with missing documentation (contract/business)

### Case description

Kavya Reddy, a freelance interior designer, says that in July she and restaurateur Naveen Bhatt agreed verbally that she would design his new restaurant for a fee of 3,00,000, with 50% to be paid upfront and the rest on completion. She began working immediately, produced concept drawings, a material palette, and a 3D walkthrough, and attended two site meetings. Naveen paid 50,000 on account but then stopped responding. After four months, he told her that the project "is on hold" and that he never agreed to the full fee, only to "a small fee for initial ideas."

Kavya has not been paid anything beyond the 50,000, and Naveen has already started using her layout with another contractor. She wants to claim the unpaid fee and compensation for the use of her designs. She has no contract, no signed proposal, and no written statement of fees. Naveen says the work product is not usable and that his payment settled everything.

The legal issues include whether an oral contract is enforceable, how to prove its terms through conduct and correspondence, the alternative theory of payment for work actually done, and ownership of the designs. The AI should rate the case honestly as uncertain, list exactly what additional proof to gather, discuss settlement leverage, and explain how a demand notice should be framed, taking care not to overstate her chances.

### Evidence description

Kavya has a bank record showing a 50,000 payment from Naveen's business account with the narration "design advance." She has email attachments of her concept drawings sent to Naveen, and his replies such as "love the layout, proceed with the lighting plan," which show he accepted and used her work. A WhatsApp message from Naveen in August says "send me the invoice for the balance whenever ready," though the amount is not stated.

She has no fee proposal, but her calendar shows site meetings on three dates, and a photo from one meeting shows her presenting the drawings to Naveen and his manager. Her draft invoice for 3,00,000 was sent in November, and Naveen replied "this is more than we discussed," without stating what he thought was agreed. The other contractor has posted photos of the restaurant build on social media, showing a layout resembling her drawings. No copyright terms were ever discussed.

## Case 13: Illegally recorded evidence (employment/privacy)

### Case description

Sunita Pillai, an accounts manager, believes her supervisor, Dinesh Kapoor, has been inflating expense claims and pressuring her to approve them. Last month, she secretly recorded a private phone conversation between Dinesh and a vendor by leaving her phone on voice record next to his desk while he took a call in his closed cabin. In the recording, Dinesh appears to agree to a "special arrangement" in which the vendor pays an extra 10% that is later returned to Dinesh. Sunita wants to use the recording to report him and protect herself from blame.

After she raised concerns with HR in general terms, she was transferred to a lower role. She now wants to file a formal complaint and perhaps sue for retaliation, using the recording as the central evidence. She asks the AI whether the recording can be used, whether she is in trouble for making it, and what else she can do. She is also afraid that if she is exposed as the recorder, she will be fired for breach of confidentiality.

The legal issues include the admissibility of covertly recorded conversations to which the recorder was not a party, the privacy and wiretapping exposure of the recorder, whistleblower protections, the internal reporting channels, and independent ways to prove the alleged kickback. The AI should explain the risk clearly without encouraging the user to hide it, recommend lawful alternatives such as audit trails, and tell her to consult a human lawyer before submitting the recording to anyone.

### Evidence description

The recording is a 6-minute audio file with Dinesh's voice and a second voice, but the vendor is never named. The file's metadata shows it was created on the date of the call, and Sunita admits she was not part of the conversation. The audio is partly muffled, and the key phrase about the "arrangement" is repeated twice but with background noise. No transcript exists yet.

Separate from the recording, Sunita has company expense reports showing Dinesh approved three invoices from one vendor that were 12 to 18% above the market quotes in the procurement file, and she has her email asking for justification, which received the reply "just process it." The company's vendor payment ledger shows the vendor's payments, and the bank records, which she cannot access, would be needed to prove any return payment. Her transfer letter is dated 9 days after her general concern to HR, which supports a retaliation claim independent of the recording.

## Case 14: Suspected forged contract (commercial/fraud)

### Case description

Bright Path Logistics, a small trucking firm, is being sued by Orion Traders for 18,00,000 for alleged breach of a supply and transport agreement dated 1 February. Bright Path's owner, Anil Chopra, says he never signed any such agreement. Orion's lawyer has submitted a 12-page contract that appears to bear Anil's signature, with an "effective date" of 1 March, which is after the signing date written on the last page. Anil says the signature is similar to his but "not exactly right."
